How often to update content for AI SEO depends on how fast the page's facts change. A page whose prices or versions changed six times last year needs a check every month. A page whose facts never moved needs one look a year. On top of any schedule, update a page the day one of its facts changes.

No AI engine publishes a refresh schedule, so any fixed rule, like "every 30 days," is a guess. What the data does show is that engines lean toward recently updated pages. [SE Ranking found](https://seranking.com/blog/how-to-optimize-for-chatgpt/) that pages updated in the past three months averaged 6.0 ChatGPT citations, against 3.6 for others. That's a correlation, and our guide to [the freshness factor in AI search](/blog/freshness-factor-ai-search) shows why it's strongest on time-sensitive questions.

This post answers how often to update content one page at a time. It gives you a test to measure how often a page's facts changed last year, the triggers that make a page due now, and a clear line between a real update and a cosmetic one. For review dates across a whole site, with a free spreadsheet, use our [AI search content refresh calendar](/blog/ai-search-content-refresh-calendar).

## Key Takeaways

- How often to update content depends on each page's change rate: how many of its facts changed in the last 12 months.
- The Change-Rate Test turns that count into an interval: 365 ÷ (changes + 1), rounded down to 30, 60, 90, 180 or 365 days.
- Triggers beat the clock. A price change, a new version, a replaced statistic or a rival's launch makes a page due today.
- A real update changes what a reader, or an AI answer quoting the page, would learn. Typos, styling and new dates alone don't count.
- Google warns against changing dates without substantial changes, and no AI engine documents rewarding cosmetic edits.

## Why a Fixed Schedule Is the Wrong Answer

Most advice on how often to update content gives one number for every page. That wastes effort in two directions. You re-check stable guides that were fine, and you leave fast-moving pages wrong between reviews.

The evidence points to fit, not frequency. In a [July 2026 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15771) of four Chinese-language AI search engines, the median age of dated cited pages was 18 days for time-sensitive queries but 101 days for stable ones. Across about 17 million citations, [Ahrefs found](https://ahrefs.com/blog/do-ai-assistants-prefer-to-cite-fresh-content/) the average page AI assistants cite is still 2.9 years old. Old pages keep winning questions that haven't changed.

Google says the same about its own results. Its [freshness systems](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ranking-systems-guide) favor fresher content only "for queries where it would be expected." And its helpful content guidance warns that churning content to seem fresh won't help: "No, it won't."

So the answer to how often to update content starts with the page's facts. Measure how fast they move, and set the review clock from that.

## The Change-Rate Test: Measure How Often a Page's Facts Changed

The Change-Rate Test is our five-step method for deciding how often to update content on one page. It uses last year's history as the best guess for next year's.

1. **List the perishable facts.** Go through the page and note every claim that could stop being true: prices, plan limits, version numbers, statistics, product and company names, rules, screenshots and dated examples.
2. **Find each fact's history for the last 12 months.** Check your own release notes and pricing history, the vendor's changelog, and the source of each statistic. For pages you don't own, the Internet Archive's [Wayback Machine](https://help.archive.org/help/using-the-wayback-machine/) shows saved copies of a URL over a date range, so you can see when a rival's pricing page changed.
3. **Count the changes (C).** Count every change that would have made your page wrong, whether or not you caught it at the time.
4. **Set the interval.** Divide 365 by (C + 1), then round down to the nearest of 30, 60, 90, 180 or 365 days.
5. **Recount once a year.** A page's change rate drifts as products and markets settle or speed up.

The "+ 1" gives a page with no changes a yearly check instead of none. Rounding down keeps the clock slightly ahead of the facts.

| Fact changes in the last 12 months | 365 ÷ (C + 1)    | Review every |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------ |
| 0                                  | 365 days         | 365 days     |
| 1                                  | 182 days         | 180 days     |
| 2–3                                | 91–122 days      | 90 days      |
| 4–5                                | 61–73 days       | 60 days      |
| 6 or more                          | 52 days or fewer | 30 days      |

If a page changed 12 or more times, don't shorten the clock below 30 days. A page that moves that fast needs triggers, covered in the next section, more than a tighter timer.

### Worked example: four Tallyfold pages

Tallyfold is a fictional invoicing app for agencies, with fictional rivals Brindlework and Kestrelyn. Its content lead ran the Change-Rate Test on four pages. The counts are made up for the example.

| Page                                              | What changed in the last 12 months                                                                        | C   | 365 ÷ (C + 1) | Review every |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ------------- | ------------ |
| Invoicing software pricing for agencies, compared | Tallyfold's price twice, Brindlework's plans three times, Kestrelyn's price once and its seat limit twice | 8   | 40.6          | 30 days      |
| Connect Tallyfold to your accounting tool         | Two API versions, a renamed settings menu, a new sync field                                               | 4   | 73.0          | 60 days      |
| Agency late payment statistics                    | Two annual surveys published new editions, one source moved                                               | 3   | 91.3          | 90 days      |
| What does net 30 mean?                            | Nothing                                                                                                   | 0   | 365.0         | 365 days     |

Together, those four pages need about 23 reviews a year: 12.2 + 6.1 + 4.1 + 1.0 = 23.4. Most of that load sits on one page, the pricing comparison, which is also where a wrong fact costs the most. That's the point of deciding how often to update content page by page: review time goes where facts actually move.

The Change-Rate Test gives each page its own starting interval from real history. The two work together: measure first, then let the calendar keep the dates.

## Triggers That Make a Page Due Today

A clock catches changes nobody announced. Triggers catch the ones you can see coming, and they override whatever you decided about how often to update content. When one fires, update the page that week, whatever its interval says.

| Trigger                                   | Example                                     | Where to watch for it                            |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Your own price or plan changes            | A new tier, a raised seat limit             | Your pricing and billing release notes           |
| A new version ships, or a feature retires | An API version bump, a removed export       | Your changelog and your partners' changelogs     |
| A cited statistic gets a new edition      | An annual survey publishes its 2026 numbers | The publisher's research page, each year         |
| A cited source moves or disappears        | A study link returns a 404                  | A monthly broken-link check                      |
| A rival launches, renames or reprices     | A competitor adds a free plan               | Rivals' pricing pages and product blogs          |
| An AI answer quotes the page wrong        | ChatGPT repeats last year's price           | Your prompt panel, or buyer and support reports  |
| The page's AI visibility drops            | Fewer citations in Bing's AI report         | Bing Webmaster Tools and Search Console, monthly |

The last two triggers are the AI-specific ones. If an assistant repeats an old claim from your page, that page jumps the queue; our guide to [fixing incorrect brand facts in AI answers](/blog/fix-incorrect-brand-facts-in-ai-answers) traces a wrong answer back to its source. Bing's [AI Performance report](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/February-2026/Introducing-AI-Performance-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Public-Preview) shows citation counts by page, and Search Console's [generative AI report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139) shows AI Overviews and AI Mode impressions by page. A page that slides in both is worth a look even if its clock isn't due.

## What Counts as an Update to Google and AI Engines

Knowing how often to update content only helps if you know what an update is. Here's the test we use: an edit is a real update if a returning reader would learn something new, or if an AI answer that quotes the page would now say something different. Everything else is maintenance.

### What Google says

Google gives three useful lines, each from its own documentation:

- Its [sitemap guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/build-sitemap) treats changes to "the main content, the structured data, or links on the page" as generally significant for `lastmod`, but not a copyright date change.
- Its [byline date guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/publication-dates) says Google estimates when a page was "published or significantly updated" from several factors, and that your dates must describe the page itself.
- Its [helpful content guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether you're "changing the date of pages to make them seem fresh when the content has not substantially changed."

### What AI engines can see

No AI engine publishes a definition of an update. What they document are the signals they read. Bing uses accurate `lastmod` values, ETags and [IndexNow](/glossary/indexnow) pings to detect changes, and its [Webmaster Guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a) say timely notices help "reduce outdated or incorrect URL references in Copilot responses." Anthropic's [web search tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/web-search-tool) passes Claude each result's `page_age`. Perplexity's Search API can [filter results](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/search/filters/date-time-filters) by last-updated date.

So engines see your dates, not your intent. A lab study from [Waseda University](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.11353) found that adding newer "Published on" dates to otherwise unchanged passages moved them up when seven LLMs reranked them. That is why a date change without new content is a trust risk rather than a shortcut: it tells engines something that isn't true.

### Real updates versus cosmetic edits

| Edit                                                       | Real update?                             | Move the visible date and `dateModified`? | Ping IndexNow and update `lastmod`?   |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| A current price, statistic or version replaces an old one  | Yes                                      | Yes                                       | Yes                                   |
| A new section answers a question people now ask            | Yes                                      | Yes                                       | Yes                                   |
| Outdated advice or a retired feature is removed            | Yes                                      | Yes                                       | Yes                                   |
| A broken source link is swapped for a live one, same claim | Partly: links changed, the answer didn't | No                                        | Yes, since Google counts link changes |
| Sentences reworded, same facts                             | No                                       | No                                        | No                                    |
| Typo, layout, template or footer year                      | No                                       | No                                        | No                                    |
| Only the date or the title's year changed                  | No, and it misleads                      | No                                        | No                                    |

When you do make a real update, change the facts first, then the dates, then tell the engines. Our [AI SEO checklist for old posts](/blog/ai-seo-checklist) covers those recrawl steps in order. To add a new fact to one section without rewriting the page, use the fact patch in our [freshness factor guide](/blog/freshness-factor-ai-search).

## How Long an Update Takes to Show Up

Whatever you choose for how often to update content, plan for weeks between an edit and a changed AI answer. Google says recrawling [can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/ask-google-to-recrawl). OpenAI's [crawler docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots) say robots.txt changes take about 24 hours to reach ChatGPT search, but a changed page still has to be fetched again and weighed against other sources.

Citations also move on their own. [Scrunch and Stacker tracked](https://scrunch.com/blog/half-life-of-ai-citations) 3.5 million citation events from September 2025 to March 2026 and found that half of a week's cited sources had dropped out after about 4.5 weeks. That's rotation, not a schedule you must match, but it means one good week proves little. Compare four weeks before an update with four weeks after, as our guide to [measuring GEO](/blog/how-to-measure-geo) describes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there a rule for how often to update content for AI search?

No engine publishes one. The practical rule is to match each page's review interval to how often its facts changed last year: monthly for pages with six or more changes, quarterly for two or three, yearly for none. Update any page immediately when one of its facts changes.

### Do I need to update content every 30 days for AI citations?

Only for pages whose facts change that often, like pricing and comparison pages. SE Ranking found pages updated within three months earned more ChatGPT citations, but Ahrefs found the average cited page is 2.9 years old. Stable guides don't need monthly edits.

### Does updating old content help AI citations?

Often, when the update adds current facts. Studies by SE Ranking and Ahrefs link recent or recently updated pages to more AI citations, though both show correlation, not cause. An update that only changes the date adds nothing, and Google warns against it.

### What counts as a content update for Google?

A change a reader would notice: new or corrected facts, new sections, removed outdated claims, or new sources. Google's sitemap guidance treats main-content, structured-data and link changes as significant, and says a copyright date change is not.

### Should I change the published date when I update a page?

Keep the original published date and add a "Last updated" date, with matching `datePublished` and `dateModified` markup. Move the updated date only after a substantial change. Google's byline guidance supports showing both dates.

### How do I know how often to update content on a specific page?

Run the Change-Rate Test. Count how many of the page's facts changed in the last 12 months, then divide 365 by that count plus one and round down to 30, 60, 90, 180 or 365 days. Recount each year.

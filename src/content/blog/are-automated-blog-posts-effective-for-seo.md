Sometimes. Automated blog posts can rank on Google and get cited by AI engines when each post adds something a reader can't get elsewhere and a person checks it before it goes live. They fail, and can drag a whole site down, when automation is used to publish many interchangeable pages mainly to rank. Google calls that scaled content abuse.

Google's own summary is blunt. "Using AI doesn't give content any special gains. It's just content," its Search team wrote in its [guidance on AI-generated content](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/02/google-search-and-ai-content). Large studies fit that picture: AI-heavy pages do rank, but on average they rank lower and earn fewer impressions than pages people wrote or heavily edited.

## Key Takeaways

- Google doesn't penalize automated blog posts for being automated or AI-written. It acts on pages made at scale mainly to manipulate rankings, "no matter how it's created."
- As of 28 September 2026, the first example in Google's scaled content abuse policy is "Using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users."
- Google has released four spam updates in 2026. The latest began rolling out on 24 September and may take up to two weeks.
- In Ahrefs' July 2026 study, 5.3% of top-three pages were fully AI-generated, but pages with low or moderate AI content earned 2–3 times the impressions of AI-heavy pages.
- "Automated" covers five different practices. The risk sits in volume without value, not in the tool.
- Run the Keep-or-Cut Test below on every automated post before it goes live, and publish no faster than you can review.

## What Counts as Automated Blog Posts?

People mean very different things by "automated blog posts." Google treats them differently too, so it helps to name which one you're asking about.

| Type of automation | What it means | What Google says | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AI drafting with human editing | A model writes the draft; a person checks facts, adds experience and edits | AI is useful "when researching a topic, and to add structure to original content," per its [generative AI guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) | Low, if the review is real |
| Fully AI-generated, unreviewed | Drafts go live as written | Many such pages "without adding value for users" is the first scaled content abuse example | High at volume |
| Data-driven pages | Pages built from your own data: prices, stats, schedules | Automation "has long been used to generate helpful content, such as sports scores, weather forecasts, and transcripts" | Low if each page has real data; see [programmatic SEO](/glossary/programmatic-seo) |
| Scheduled publishing | Only the timing is automated | Google doesn't favor sites for publishing more often; John Mueller said in 2018 that "a site isn't a machine that pumps out content at a fixed rate," [per Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-content-frequency-25367.html) | No gain or penalty by itself |
| Scraped, spun or auto-translated | Other people's content reworded at scale | Named in the spam policy: "synonymizing, translating, or other obfuscation techniques" | Very high |

So the honest answer depends on the row. Scheduling is neutral. Drafting with AI is fine if a person does the work of checking and adding. The trouble starts when the draft is the final product and there are hundreds of them.

## What Google Says About Automated Blog Posts

Google's position hasn't changed much since 2023. What has changed is the spam policy's wording, which since March 2024 targets content made at scale however it's produced.

### "It's just content": the 2023 guidance

Google's February 2023 post says "Appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines." It adds that AI content "might do well in Search" if it is "useful, helpful, original, and satisfies aspects of E-E-A-T. If it doesn't, it might not." E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust; see our [E-E-A-T entry](/glossary/e-e-a-t).

The same post gives a one-line test for intent: "If you see AI as an inexpensive, easy way to game search engine rankings, then no." On labels, Google says AI disclosures are "useful for content where someone might think 'How was this created?'" They're encouraged, not required.

### The scaled content abuse policy, as of 28 September 2026

Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) page was last updated on 28 August 2026. It defines scaled content abuse as "when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users." It is "typically focused on creating large amounts of unoriginal content that provides little to no value to users, no matter how it's created." The listed examples are:

1. Using generative AI or similar tools to produce many pages that add no value for users.
2. Scraping feeds, search results or other content to make many pages, including by synonymizing, translating or other obfuscation.
3. Stitching together content from different pages without adding value.
4. Creating multiple sites to hide how much content is being scaled.
5. Creating many pages that make little sense to a reader but contain search keywords.

Two details are easy to miss. The page now defines spam to include "attempting to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search," which reaches answers in features such as AI Overviews and AI Mode. And it says Google detects violations "through automated systems and, as needed, human review that can result in a manual action." Violating sites "may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all." Our glossary covers [scaled content abuse](/glossary/scaled-content-abuse) in more depth.

### The questions Google asks about automation

Google's [people-first content page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) lists warning signs. Two bear on automation directly: "Are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?" and "Are you producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results?"

Its generative AI page also points to the Search Quality Rater Guidelines, section 4.6.5 on scaled content abuse and section 4.6.6 on content with "little to no effort, little to no originality, and little to no added value." Google says rater scores don't directly change rankings, but the guidelines show what its systems are built to recognize.

### Spam updates keep coming in 2026

Google's [Search Status Dashboard](https://status.search.google.com/incidents/XhUDXP7A67iHCD2kmbVu) lists spam updates on 24 March, 24 June and 18 August 2026. A fourth, the September 2026 spam update, was released on 24 September and "applies globally and to all languages." As of 28 September 2026 it's still rolling out, which can take up to two weeks. If you publish automated blog posts at volume, watch Search Console closely until it finishes.

## What the Evidence Shows About Automated Blog Posts

Google's rules tell you where the line is. Studies tell you how automated blog posts and other AI-heavy pages perform on each side of it. All of them use AI detectors, which make mistakes, and all of them show correlation, not cause.

### Ahrefs: no penalty, but a clear slope

In 2025, [Ahrefs checked 600,000 pages](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-generated-content-does-not-hurt-your-google-rankings/) ranking in the top 20 for 100,000 random keywords. Only 13.5% scored as purely human-written, 4.6% as purely AI and 81.9% as a mix. The correlation between AI share and ranking position was 0.011, which is effectively none.

A [follow-up in July 2026](https://ahrefs.com/blog/google-doesnt-punish-ai-content/) looked closer and found a gentle slope. Some fully AI pages reach the top: 5.3% of pages in positions one to three scored as 100% AI. But performance fell as the AI share rose:

- **Indexing:** 49.28% of low-AI pages were indexed, against 40.35% of very-high-AI pages.
- **Impressions:** low- and moderate-AI pages earned 2–3 times the impressions of high-AI pages.
- **Top spots:** pages under 50% AI content held 82.2% of top-three rankings.

Ahrefs saw no sign of a switch that blocks AI content. Its author's reading is that AI use tends to go with lower quality, and quality is what Google measures.

### Graphite: people-written pages hold the top spots

[Graphite's October 2025 study](https://graphite.io/five-percent/ai-content-in-search-and-llms) of Google results for 31,493 keywords found 86% of ranking articles were human-written. In ChatGPT and Perplexity, 82% of cited articles were human-written. Only 7% of the articles in position one were AI-generated. Graphite didn't test AI drafts that people heavily edited, and says that approach "may be an effective strategy."

Newer data is less tidy. For a set of questions mostly about named products and people, Graphite found that 42.7% of ChatGPT's cited pages in June 2026 were [predicted to be AI-generated](https://graphite.io/five-percent/research/ai-search-collapse). Different questions and detectors give different shares.

### What a scaled-content crash looks like

Consultant Glenn Gabe calls the pattern "Mt. AI": traffic climbs fast on scaled AI content, then falls hard. In an [April 2026 case study](https://www.gsqi.com/marketing-blog/when-mt-ai-crumbles-chatgpt-follows/), he described a site whose local news directory had over 850,000 indexed URLs of fully AI-generated articles. After a manual action for scaled content abuse, the section dropped out of Google, and its visibility in AI Overviews, AI Mode and ChatGPT citations fell with it. Engines that ground answers in search results can lose you when search does.

## The Keep-or-Cut Test for Automated Blog Posts

The Keep-or-Cut Test is our eight-question check for any post that a tool drafted or scheduled. Answer yes or no for each. It takes about ten minutes a post once you're used to it.

1. **Real question.** Would a customer actually ask this? Check Search Console, sales calls or support tickets.
2. **Something new.** Does the post say at least one thing the top five results don't? See [information gain](/glossary/information-gain).
3. **Your own fact.** Does it include a fact only your company can supply, such as a price, a spec or a test result?
4. **Checked by a person.** Has someone who knows the subject verified every claim, number and link?
5. **No twin.** Is this the only page on your site that answers this question?
6. **Sources inline.** Does every statistic link to where it came from?
7. **Clear owner.** Is it clear who stands behind it, with a disclosure where a reader would ask "How was this created?"
8. **Pace you can review.** Are you publishing no faster than your team can check?

Score automated blog posts the same way every time: 7–8 yes means publish. 5–6 means fix the gaps first. 4 or fewer means cut it, merge it into a stronger page, or keep it out of search.

### Worked example: Tallyfold's first 30 drafts

Tallyfold is a made-up invoicing and payments app for agencies. Its team sets up a tool to draft 30 automated blog posts a month and runs the Keep-or-Cut Test on the first batch.

| Result | Drafts | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Publish (7–8 yes) | 12 | Went live after a final read |
| Fix (5–6 yes) | 11 | All 11 failed check 3; 9 passed after the team added plan limits and a sync spec, and 2 still had no first-party fact |
| Cut (4 or fewer) | 7 | 5 duplicated existing Tallyfold pages; 2 covered topics its customers never ask about |

The totals: 12 + 9 = 21 posts published, and 7 + 2 = 9 cut or merged, for 30 drafts. The 21 automated blog posts that shipped each carry at least one fact only Tallyfold holds. The most common failure wasn't bad writing. It was check 3, failed by every draft in the fix pile: a draft with no first-party fact reads fine and adds nothing. Tallyfold and its numbers are illustrative.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Google penalize AI-generated blog posts?

No, Google doesn't penalize posts for being AI-generated. It says "Appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines." It does act on scaled content abuse: many pages made mainly to manipulate rankings, whether by AI, templates or people. Studies find AI-heavy pages rank lower on average, which looks like a quality effect rather than a detector.

### What is scaled content abuse?

Scaled content abuse is Google's spam policy against "many pages" generated "for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users." It covers AI, scraping, stitching and template methods alike. Sites that violate it may rank lower or drop out of results, and Google can apply a manual action for it.

### How many automated blog posts can I publish per day?

Google sets no safe number for automated blog posts. What matters is whether each post helps readers and whether your team can review everything it publishes. Google's own warning signs include "producing lots of content on many different topics," so pace publishing to your review capacity, not to what a tool can generate.

### Should I label automated blog posts as AI-generated?

A label is optional but often wise. Google says disclosures are "useful for content where someone might think 'How was this created?'" and suggests explaining how automation was used. It also says listing AI as the author is "probably not the best way" to disclose. A named human editor plus a short note works well.

### Do ChatGPT and Perplexity cite AI-written blog posts?

Yes, sometimes. Graphite's October 2025 study found 18% of articles cited by ChatGPT and Perplexity were AI-generated. A June 2026 Graphite study found 42.7% for a set of mostly product and entity questions. Engines cite pages that answer the question well, so the same quality rules apply to automated blog posts.

### Can a site recover from a scaled content abuse penalty?

Yes, but it takes work. For a manual action, fix every affected page, then use Request Review in Search Console's Manual Actions report. Google says most reviews "can take several days or weeks," per its [manual actions help page](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044175). After an algorithmic spam update, consultant [Glenn Gabe reports](https://www.gsqi.com/marketing-blog/august-2026-google-spam-update-case-studies/) that recovery usually takes months of clean publishing.

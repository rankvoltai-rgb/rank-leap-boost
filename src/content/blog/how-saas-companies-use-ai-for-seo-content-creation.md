SaaS companies use AI across the whole SEO content workflow, but mostly around the writing rather than in place of it. Teams use it to sort customer questions into topics, build briefs, produce first drafts, refresh old posts, suggest internal links and translate. People keep the jobs AI does worst, which in software means product facts, first-hand experience and the final edit.

Adoption is close to universal, and results are not. Among the 266 technology marketers in the Content Marketing Institute's [2026 tech research](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/technology-research/content-marketing-technology-research), 95% of those using AI apply it to written content. Yet only 35% of the tech marketers using AI for content said content performance had improved, and 18% said quality had dropped. How SaaS companies use AI, not whether they use it, decides which side of that split they land on.

Content matters more now that buyers' AI agents read SaaS sites before people do, a shift we cover in [the prompt-zero purchase](/blog/prompt-zero-purchase-ai-agents-buy-software). This post stays practical: the workflow stage by stage, what published surveys show, where Google draws the line, and how a fictional SaaS team runs it.

## Key Takeaways

- The most common AI content tasks are brainstorming (76%), outlines (73%) and updating content (67%), per Ahrefs' 2025 survey of 879 marketers. Translation (22%) and keyword clustering (23%) trail.
- Almost no one publishes raw output. In the same survey, 97% had a review process, and only 4% published mostly unedited AI text.
- Speed is the reliable gain. Among tech marketers using AI for content, 90% saw productivity improve, but only 35% saw performance improve (CMI, November 2025).
- Orbit Media's 2026 survey of 1,042 content marketers found AI use "correlates with nothing" when it comes to results, while formal human editing and original research did.
- Google accepts AI-assisted content that helps people, and treats "many pages without adding value" as scaled content abuse.
- In SaaS, the facts that decide deals (prices, limits, integrations) must come from your product team, never from a model's memory.

## How SaaS Companies Use AI at Each Stage

The table maps where SaaS companies use AI in a typical SEO content workflow, what the tool does well, and what a person should keep. The survey shares come from Ahrefs' [June 2025 study of 879 marketers](https://ahrefs.com/blog/marketers-using-ai-publish-more-content/). Ahrefs sells SEO software, so treat it as a survey of its own audience.

| Stage                       | What AI does well                                       | What a person owns                          | Marketers using AI here                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Topic and question research | Groups tickets, call notes and queries into themes      | Deciding which questions matter to buyers   | 76% (brainstorming)                    |
| Briefs and outlines         | Proposes headings, angles and sources to check          | The angle, and the product facts            | 73%                                    |
| First drafts                | Writes a draft from the brief and the sources           | Experience, examples, opinions, screenshots | Blog posts are the top AI format (87%) |
| Editing                     | Tightens wording and structure                          | Checking every claim, number and link       | 80% review manually                    |
| Refreshes                   | Flags pages that mention changed features or old prices | Deciding what changed, then rewriting       | 67% (improving content)                |
| Internal links              | Suggests related pages to link                          | Approving anchors and targets               | Not measured                           |
| Translation                 | First-pass translation                                  | A native speaker's review                   | 22%                                    |
| Metadata                    | Drafts titles, meta descriptions and alt text           | Accuracy                                    | Not measured                           |

### Research: start from what customers already ask

When SaaS companies use AI for research, they start with better question data than most publishers have. Support tickets, sales-call notes, onboarding chats and community threads hold the exact words buyers use. A model can cluster a few hundred of them into themes in minutes. A person then picks the themes that match a buying decision, not just the loudest complaint.

Strip names, emails and account details before any of it goes into an outside tool. If you're starting from zero, our free [AI question generator](/tools/ai-question-generator) suggests questions buyers ask AI assistants about a topic.

### Drafts: product facts come from the product team

This is where things go wrong most often when SaaS companies use AI. A model writes smoothly about your product from whatever it absorbed in training, which may be last year's pricing or a rival's feature. In G2's [March 2026 survey](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/new-g2-research-half-of-b2b-software-buyers-now-start-their-research-with-ai-chatbots-302742807.html), 64% of software buyers said they see inaccurate AI chatbot recommendations "often or very often." Your own blog shouldn't add to the pile.

The fix is a fact sheet: current prices, plan limits, integrations and what each one syncs, supported regions, and what the product doesn't do. Paste it into every brief, and ban any product number that isn't on it. Our post on [hallucination by omission](/blog/hallucination-by-omission-pricing-page) shows why missing prices get filled in with guesses.

### Refreshes: tie content to the changelog

Software products change often, so SaaS posts can go stale quickly. Two-thirds of Ahrefs' respondents already use AI to improve existing content. When SaaS companies use AI for refreshes, the practical version is a trigger: when a release note ships, search your posts for the feature it touches, and have AI list every sentence that may now be wrong. A person rewrites. Our [content refresh calendar for AI search](/blog/ai-search-content-refresh-calendar) covers how often to check each page type.

### Translation: one language per page, with a native reviewer

Only 22% of Ahrefs' respondents used AI to localize or translate. The rest may be right to go slow. Google's [multilingual guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites) asks for a single language per page and warns against side-by-side translations. Its [spam policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) names automated translation when it's used to repackage scraped content with little value. Translating your own pages is a different case, but a native reviewer should check product terms and tone before anything ships.

## What Surveys Show About AI Content in Tech and SaaS

Three recent surveys show how tech and SaaS companies use AI for content. Each has a sponsor with its own interests, and each shows correlation, not cause.

### Adoption is near universal

- **CMI and MarketingProfs, November 2025.** The survey was fielded from June to August 2025, and its technology cut covers 266 tech marketers. Among them, 85% are past the exploratory stage with AI. The top uses are written content (95%), images and video (53%) and SEO tools (44%).
- **Ahrefs, June 2025.** 87% of 879 respondents use AI to help create content. ChatGPT was the most used tool (44%), followed by Gemini (15%) and Claude (10%).
- **Orbit Media, September 2026.** In its [survey of 1,042 content marketers](https://www.orbitmedia.com/blog/blogging-statistics/), 92.4% use AI for blogging.

### Speed is the sure gain; results are mixed

The time savings are real. Ahrefs found that companies using AI published a median of 17 articles a month, against 12 for companies that didn't. Among CMI's tech marketers using AI for content, 90% said productivity improved and 81% said operational efficiency improved.

Quality and results are another story. In the CMI tech data, 53% said content quality improved, while 18% said it got worse. Only 35% said content performance improved, and 18% said it was too soon to tell.

Orbit Media's data is the bluntest. Only 13.9% of its respondents said their blog delivers "strong results," the lowest in the survey's history, and AI use "correlates with nothing" on that measure. What did correlate: a formal editing process with human oversight, and publishing original research, which Orbit says "improves the likelihood of performance by 50%." More output is easy. Better output still takes people.

## Where Google Draws the Line

Google doesn't rank content lower for being AI-assisted. Its [guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) says AI "can be particularly useful when researching a topic, and to add structure to original content." The same page warns that using AI "to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate" the scaled content abuse policy. It also asks for accuracy in the parts people forget, including title elements, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text.

Google's [people-first content page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) suggests asking "Who, How, and Why" about each page. The "How" asks whether AI use is "self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways." The "Why" matters most: content made "primarily to attract search engine visits" isn't what Google's systems reward. For the full policy text, the 2026 spam updates and the studies on AI-heavy pages, read our answer to [whether automated blog posts are effective for SEO](/blog/are-automated-blog-posts-effective-for-seo). Our glossary entry on [scaled content abuse](/glossary/scaled-content-abuse) covers the policy in brief.

When SaaS companies use AI to scale pages, the risk isn't one AI-drafted post. It's the programmatic temptation: a page for every integration, city or job title, each one a template with a few words swapped. If the pages differ only in the noun, they fit Google's description of scaled content.

## The Tallyfold Content Relay: A Worked Team Workflow

Tallyfold is a fictional B2B invoicing and payments app for agencies. Its team, its hours and its plan below are invented to show how small SaaS companies use AI and where the time goes. None of it is measured data.

The team is small: a content lead, a product marketer who gives content a few hours a week, and a support engineer who reviews each post for an hour. The goal is eight new posts a month plus steady refreshes. We call their process the Tallyfold Content Relay: AI runs each leg, then hands off to a named person before the next leg starts.

| Leg                      | Hours per post before AI | Hours per post with AI | Who takes the handoff                                |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Question research        | 2.0                      | 0.5                    | Content lead picks themes from clustered tickets     |
| Brief                    | 1.5                      | 0.5                    | Product marketer attaches the fact sheet             |
| Draft                    | 5.0                      | 2.0                    | Content lead rewrites the AI draft and adds examples |
| Edit and fact-check      | 2.0                      | 2.0                    | Content lead checks every claim and link             |
| Expert review            | 1.0                      | 1.0                    | Support engineer signs off on product detail         |
| Links, metadata, publish | 1.0                      | 0.5                    | Content lead approves links and titles               |
| **Total**                | **12.5**                 | **6.5**                |                                                      |

The arithmetic: eight posts at 12.5 hours is 100 hours a month. At 6.5 hours, it's 52 hours. That frees 48 hours. Tallyfold spends them on three things: six refreshes a month at 3 hours each (18 hours), one original data piece built from anonymized invoice data (20 hours), and promotion (10 hours). That's 18 + 20 + 10 = 48 hours.

Two rows didn't shrink, on purpose. However SaaS companies use AI, fact-checking and expert review should keep their full length, because that's where the quality in Orbit's data comes from. And the data piece is the one thing no model can write for Tallyfold: only Tallyfold has its customers' median days to payment.

### The relay rules

1. **No product number without the fact sheet.** Prices, limits and integration details come from the product team.
2. **Every statistic links to its source.** If the source can't be found, the sentence goes.
3. **An expert signs off on how the product works.** The support engineer reads every post.
4. **Publish only as fast as you can review.** Eight reviewed posts beat 30 unreviewed ones.
5. **Refresh on release.** Each release note triggers a search of old posts.
6. **Disclose where a reader would ask.** Say how AI was used when someone might wonder how the page was made.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do SaaS companies use AI for SEO content?

Mostly for research, outlines, first drafts and updating old posts, with people handling product facts, editing and expert review. Across marketers in general, Ahrefs' 2025 survey found the top uses were brainstorming (76%), outlining (73%) and improving existing content (67%), and very few teams publish unedited AI text.

### Does Google penalize AI-written SaaS blog posts?

No, not for being AI-written. Google's guidance says AI can help with research and structure. It acts against "many pages" made without adding value for users, which its spam policy calls scaled content abuse. A reviewed, useful post is fine however it was drafted.

### Which content tasks should SaaS companies use AI for, and which should stay human?

Use AI for question research, outlines, first drafts, refresh flags and metadata drafts. Keep product facts, fact-checking, expert review and original data with people. Orbit Media's 2026 survey tied strong results to formal human editing and original research, not to AI use itself.

### How many AI-assisted posts can a small SaaS team publish?

As many as it can review properly. Ahrefs found a median of 17 articles a month at companies using AI, against 12 without it. In our fictional Tallyfold example, AI cut drafting time, but fact-checks and expert review kept their full hours.

### Should SaaS companies disclose AI-written content?

Google suggests it where readers might ask "How was this created?" It's encouraged, not required. In Ahrefs' survey, only 16% of companies using AI said they disclose it. A short note on method builds trust on data-heavy or advice pages.

### Can AI translate SaaS content for SEO?

Yes, as a first draft. Google asks for one language per page and warns against side-by-side translations. Have a native speaker review product terms and tone before publishing, and avoid translating only the page template while the content stays in one language.
